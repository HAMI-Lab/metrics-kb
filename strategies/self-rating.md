# Self-rating: questionnaires and rating scales

> People rate their own experience or judgment on a scale, and the ratings are scored.

## 1. What it is

### 1.1 Definition
The person who used the system answers items on a rating scale. Responses are scored into a number by a fixed rule.

### 1.2 Typical research methods
Post-task and post-study questionnaires, surveys, diary entries, and experience-sampling prompts during the day [bolger2003].

### 1.3 Named practices
- **Standardized questionnaires** with fixed scoring, such as SUS [brooke1996] and NASA-TLX [hart1988].
- **Single-item post-task ratings** (for example, perceived difficulty).
- **Experience sampling** ratings collected in the moment over days or weeks [bolger2003].

## 2. When to use it
Use it when the construct is perceived (satisfaction, perceived usability, workload as experienced). It is a weak substitute for observing behaviour or outcomes.

## 3. Shared caveats

### 3.1 Validity: how the number can miss the construct
Direct for perceived constructs, proxy for objective ones. The same construct measured by questionnaire and by behaviour often disagrees, partly because the method itself contributes variance [campbell1959].

### 3.2 Reliability
Check internal consistency for multi-item scales and test-retest stability. Means from small samples have wide intervals.

### 3.3 Bias and gaming
Reactive: being asked changes what people report [webb1966]. Social desirability toward the researcher, recency (the last task dominates), and acquiescence (agreeing with items) bias scores. Scores can be inflated by choosing friendly respondents or easy tasks.

## 4. Cost
Low per respondent (minutes), but needs people who actually used the system; cannot be computed from logs.
