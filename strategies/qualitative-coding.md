# Coding qualitative data

> Interviews, diaries, observations, or open-ended answers are coded into categories, and the codes are counted.

## 1. What it is

### 1.1 Definition
Researchers assign codes from a codebook to segments of qualitative data, then count codes per participant, session, or task.

### 1.2 Typical research methods
Interviews, diary studies [bolger2003], observation and think-aloud, and open-ended survey answers [lazar2017].

### 1.3 Named practices
- **Content analysis** with a fixed codebook and counts [krippendorff2004].
- **Usability problem counts** from think-aloud or observation, often with severity ratings.
- **Critical incident counts** from diaries or interviews (for example, times work was lost).
- **Thematic analysis** [braun2006] usually does not produce numbers; counting themes is possible but should be done with care.

## 2. When to use it
Use it when the construct is not yet logged or asked about in a fixed form, or to check what users actually experience. More often, qualitative methods serve roles around a metric rather than producing it: discovering which constructs matter (construct pages, section 6), checking validity (metric pages, section 2.1), and explaining why a metric moved (section 5.3).

## 3. Shared caveats

### 3.1 Validity: how the number can miss the construct
The codebook decides what is counted; a code that is easy to apply may not be the construct. Samples are usually small, so counts are imprecise.

### 3.2 Reliability
Measure agreement between independent coders, for example with Krippendorff's alpha [krippendorff2004], before using counts.

### 3.3 Bias and gaming
Coders' expectations shape coding; blind coding to condition helps. Participants in interviews may tell researchers what they think they want to hear.

## 4. Cost
High per data point: collection, transcription, and coding by trained people.
